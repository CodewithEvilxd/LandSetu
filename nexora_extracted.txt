LandSetu: An AI-Driven National Land Governance Intelligence Platform 
Integrating Heterogeneous Land Records and Public Grievances for Evidence-
Based Policy Decision-Making
Kumari Akashi Jaiswal, Neha, Nishant Gaurav
Department of Computer Science and Engineering (Data Science)
Noida Institute of Engineering and Technology (NIET), Greater Noida, India
Team NEXORA — IEEE Student Branch, NIET, Research-A-Thon 2026

Live Prototype: sih-proto-frontend.vercel.app
Abstract—Every year, millions of Indian families wait for a single stamped signature to tell them a piece of land is truly theirs 
— yet land-related litigation is estimated to occupy a majority share of pending civil cases in Indian courts, even as over 95% of 
rural land records now sit digitized in government servers. The paradox is structural: land revenue departments, survey and  
settlement  records,  geospatial  cadastral  datasets,  and  citizen  grievance  platforms  operate  as  isolated  silos,  leaving  
administrators unable to see, in one place, which regions suffer recurring disputes, which mutation requests are stuck the  
longest, or which policy gaps are driving citizen dissatisfaction. This paper presents LandSetu, an AI-driven national land  
governance intelligence platform that harmonizes structured land records with unstructured grievance narratives through entity 
resolution,  geospatial  alignment,  OCR-based  document  digitization,  and  natural  language  processing.  The  platform's  
processing  core  combines  data  integration,  machine  learning  classification,  spatial  analysis,  retrieval-augmented  legal  
knowledge  lookup  (RAG),  and  policy-impact  simulation  to  convert  fragmented  records  into  an  evidence-based  policy 
dashboard, supported by a citizen-facing grievance portal and a real-time officer dashboard synchronized with government  
databases. A working prototype was implemented and evaluated on a small pilot sample of representative grievance records and 
scanned land documents. On this pilot sample (n = 60), the LandSetu NLP classification pipeline achieved 83.3% accuracy,  
81.0% precision, 80.0% recall, and an 80.5% F1-score, compared with 68.3% accuracy for a keyword/rule-based baseline. The 
results, while based on a small-scale pilot rather than a production deployment, indicate that integrating previously siloed land-
governance data sources through AI can meaningfully improve the detection of systemic bottlenecks and support transparent,  
targeted policy intervention.
Keywords—Land Governance, Data Integration, Natural Language Processing, Entity Resolution, Public Grievances,  
Evidence-Based Policy, Retrieval-Augmented Generation, Geospatial Analysis, e-Governance.

I. INTRODUCTION
Somewhere in rural India today, a farmer is standing in line  
outside a tehsil office for the third time this month, holding a 
mutation application that has quietly gone nowhere. Multiply 
that single wait by the estimated tens of millions of pending  
land-related  cases  clogging  Indian  courts,  and  land  
governance  stops  looking  like  a  paperwork  problem  and 
starts looking like a national productivity crisis — one that  
touches  credit  access,  agricultural  investment,  urban  
planning, and social trust in the state itself.
India has not been passive about this. The Digital India Land 
Records Modernization Programme (DILRMP), launched in  
2016, has computerized over 95% of rural land records and  
digitized a majority of cadastral maps nationwide [6]. The  
government has gone further still, assigning every land parcel 
a unique 14-digit Unique Land Parcel Identification Number 
(ULPIN) — popularly nicknamed “Bhu-Aadhaar” — so that, 
in principle, no parcel of Indian soil need ever again be  
ambiguous about who owns it [7].
And yet the disputes persist. The reason is not a lack of data  
but a lack of connection between data. Revenue records live  
in one department's database, survey and settlement maps in  
another, geospatial cadastral layers in a third, and citizen  
grievances — arguably the richest, most human signal of  
where the system is failing — sit almost entirely unread in  
free-text form on grievance portals. No administrator today  
can  easily  ask,  and  get  an  evidence-based  answer  to,  a  
question as simple as: “Which district has both slow mutation 
processing and a rising cluster of title-dispute complaints, and 
why?”
This paper introduces LandSetu, a national land governance  
and research intelligence platform designed to close exactly  
that gap. Rather than digitizing more data, LandSetu is built  
to  make  sense  of  the  data  that  already  exists  —  fusing  
structured revenue and survey records, OCR-scanned legacy 
documents,  geospatial  layers,  and  unstructured  citizen  
grievances  into  a  single  AI-driven  analytical  layer  that  
surfaces patterns no single department could see on its own.  
The remainder of this paper defines the problem precisely,  
positions LandSetu against the existing research and product 
landscape,  details  its  architecture  and  methodology,  and  
reports preliminary results from an early pilot evaluation of  
the prototype.
II. PROBLEM STATEMENT
Existing land governance systems in India digitize records  
within  isolated  departmental  silos  and  therefore  lack  a  
mechanism to correlate structured revenue/survey data with  
unstructured  citizen  grievance  narratives  at  the  parcel  or 
regional  level.  Consequently,  decision-makers  cannot  
systematically identify which specific governance processes  
are  generating  clusters  of  disputes,  which  regions  show  
abnormally  long  mutation-processing  delays,  or  which  
grievance themes require the most urgent policy attention,  
resulting  in  reactive  rather  than  evidence-based  land  
administration.
III. RESEARCH GAP
A review of the existing landscape reveals four recurring gaps 
that this work aims to address:
•  Prior GIS-based land record systems focus primarily on 
spatial visualization and title-mapping, without 
integrating citizen grievance text as an analytical input.
•  Existing grievance-analytics platforms classify and route 
complaints but rarely link them back to structured land-
parcel or survey records for root-cause analysis.
•  Few frameworks apply entity resolution to reconcile 
citizen-reported locations/parcel references with 
authoritative revenue and geospatial datasets, or use 
retrieval-augmented legal knowledge lookup to ground 
insights in actual law and precedent.
•  Most current systems produce descriptive dashboards 
(counts, status trackers) rather than correlative, 
simulation-backed, policy-actionable insights connecting 
governance-process gaps to dispute patterns.
IV. OBJECTIVES
The specific objectives of this research are:
•  To design a data harmonization framework that unifies 
structured land revenue/survey records with unstructured 
citizen grievance text and scanned legacy documents 
using entity resolution, OCR, and geospatial alignment.
•  To develop an NLP-based pipeline for grievance 
classification and theme extraction from citizen 
complaint narratives.
•  To integrate a retrieval-augmented legal knowledge layer 
capable of grounding insights in applicable laws, 
policies, and court rulings.
•  To build a correlation and policy-impact simulation 
module linking governance-process indicators with 
dispute and delay patterns.
•  To implement and evaluate a working prototype 
(LandSetu) on a representative pilot dataset using 
standard performance metrics.
V. RESEARCH QUESTIONS
RQ1: How can heterogeneous land-governance data sources 
— including scanned legacy documents — be harmonized  
into a unified, parcel-level schema using entity resolution and 
geospatial alignment?

RQ2:  How  effectively  can  transformer-based  NLP  
techniques  classify  and  extract  themes  from  unstructured 
citizen  grievance  narratives  compared  with  a  rule-based

RQ3:  What  correlations  exist  between  specific  land-

RQ4: Can a retrieval-augmented legal knowledge layer and

VI. LITERATURE REVIEW AND COMPARATIVE 
ANALYSIS
Several  recent  studies  have  explored  AI-assisted  citizen  
grievance handling. Aakash et al. proposed CivicSense, a  
generative-AI and NLP-based platform that automatically  
identifies, ranks, and summarizes large volumes of municipal 
citizen  complaints,  converting  unstructured  text  into  
structured, actionable insight for authorities [1]. Kotwal et al. 
developed  a  web-based  Grievance  Redressal  Portal  and  
explicitly noted that existing systems suffer from an absence 
of  unified  platforms  and  minimal  analytical  insight  for  
administrators, a gap this paper directly targets [2]. A study  
conducted for Nairobi County used a support vector classifier 
with  TF-IDF  features  to  categorize  social-media  civic  
complaints, achieving 77.52% classification accuracy, and  
applied named-entity recognition with reverse geocoding to  
spatially locate complaints [3]. An ACL workshop study on  
complaint analysis for economic and food-safety regulators  
applied  traditional  feature-based  classifiers  to  citizen  
feedback,  reporting  accuracy  scores  above  70%  [4].  A  
Flask/MySQL-based  AI-driven  grievance  lodging  and  
tracking system incorporated NLP purely for departmental  
routing rather than for deeper policy analytics [5].
Across this body of work, classification accuracy for citizen-
complaint text with classical machine-learning approaches  
typically  remains  in  the  70–80%  range,  and  —  more  
importantly — none of the reviewed systems link grievance  
analytics back to structured land-parcel or revenue records,  
apply legal-knowledge retrieval, or simulate the downstream 
impact  of  a  proposed  policy  change.  Table  I  positions  
LandSetu against this landscape along the feature dimensions 
that matter most for land governance specifically — shown in 
full width below for readability.

B. System Architecture
The architecture consists of three layers, shown in Fig. 2: an  
input layer, a processing layer, and an output layer.
Fig. 2.  System architecture of LandSetu (from the team's 
prototype design).
•  Input Layer: State land records (digitized land 
databases), OCR documents (scanned Jamabandi, 
Khasra, and official notifications), and public grievances 
(web, mobile, and CPGRAMS-style channels).
•  Processing Layer — AI & Analytical Framework: Data 
Integration & Preprocessing; OCR & NLP Processing; 
Machine Learning Models; Spatial & Geospatial 
Analysis; Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) for 
legal knowledge retrieval; and Policy Impact Simulation 
— informed continuously by a Knowledge Base (laws, 
policies, court rulings) and Geospatial Data (cadastral 
maps, satellite imagery).
•  Output Layer: A policy dashboard providing visual, 
evidence-based insights for decision-makers.
C. Real-Time Citizen-to-Officer Workflow
Beyond batch analytics, LandSetu supports a live operational 
loop connecting citizens, the AI risk engine, and government 
officers, illustrated in Fig. 3. A citizen submits a grievance or 
document through the Citizen Portal (step 1) and receives an 
acknowledgement and status updates (step 2). In parallel, the 
AI  Risk  Engine  continuously  synchronizes  with  the  
Government  Database  to  pull  the  latest  land  records,  
litigation data, and policy/regulatory updates (step 3) and  
pushes back processed insights and risk flags (step 4). The  
engine's data-processing, ML, and risk-scoring modules then 
generate risk analyses and recommendations for the Officer  
Dashboard  (step  5),  where  officers  visualize  risk  maps,  
prioritize  cases,  and  monitor  action  —  closing  the  loop  
between citizen input and administrative response in near real 
time.
Fig. 3.  Real-time data synchronization architecture of LandSetu.
D. Data Harmonization
Structured  records  are  aligned  to  a  common  parcel-level 
schema  keyed  conceptually  on  a  ULPIN-style  unique  
identifier.  Entity  resolution  (fuzzy  string  matching  on  
owner/plot identifiers combined with geospatial proximity  
checks) reconciles records referring to the same parcel across 
departments, while citizen grievances are geo-linked to the  
nearest matching parcel or administrative unit.
E. NLP-Based Grievance Analysis
Grievance  narratives  are  cleaned  and  passed  through  a  
transformer-based  text  classifier  fine-tuned  to  categorize  
complaints into governance-relevant classes (title dispute,  
mutation  delay,  boundary  issue,  survey  error).  A  theme-
extraction step using keyword and topic modelling surfaces  
recurring sub-issues within each category.
F. Correlation and Policy Impact Simulation
=== PAGE 5 ===
Harmonized structured data and classified grievance data are 
joined at the parcel/regional level. Statistical co-occurrence  
analysis and anomaly detection identify governance-process  
indicators that correlate with elevated dispute frequency, and 
a lightweight simulation module estimates how a proposed  
process change (e.g., additional survey staff in a district)  
might affect projected dispute volume, giving policymakers a 
before/after comparison rather than a static snapshot.
VIII. DATASET
Given the sensitivity and limited public availability of live  
land revenue databases, the prototype was evaluated on a  
small,  representative  pilot  sample  assembled  from  open  
government grievance-portal text and a limited set of scanned 
specimen  land  documents,  constructed  to  mirror  real  
DILRMP-style  field  structures  (parcel  ID,  owner  name,  
mutation status, survey number, complaint text, timestamp,  
administrative region).
•  Pilot sample size: 60 labeled grievance records across 5 
categories, plus 20 scanned specimen land documents 
(Jamabandi/Khasra-style) for OCR testing.
•  Classes: Title Dispute, Mutation Delay, Boundary Issue, 
Survey Error, Other.
•  Split: held-out evaluation on the full 60-record pilot 
sample (train/validation performed on a separate small 
development batch).
•  Preprocessing: text lower-casing, stop-word removal, 
lemmatization; numeric fields normalized; duplicate 
parcel entries de-duplicated via entity resolution.
•  Limitation: this is a small-scale pilot sample, not a live 
production feed — results should be read as an early 
proof-of-concept, discussed further in Section XIV.
IX. ALGORITHMS / TECHNICAL APPROACH
A. Entity Resolution
A hybrid approach combining Levenshtein-distance fuzzy  
string matching (names/addresses) with geospatial proximity 
scoring  (parcel  coordinates)  was  selected  because  exact-
match keys are unreliable across departmental datasets with  
inconsistent formatting; manual reconciliation was rejected  
as unscalable.
B. OCR and Grievance Classification
Scanned  documents  are  processed  with  an  OCR  engine  
before downstream NLP. Grievance text is classified using a 
fine-tuned transformer (DistilBERT-style) chosen over a TF-
IDF  +  rule-based  baseline  because  it  captures  contextual 
semantics in free-text grievances, which is important given  
inconsistent  complaint  phrasing.  Key  parameters:  max  
sequence length 128 tokens, batch size 16, learning rate 2e-5, 
4 training epochs.
C. Retrieval-Augmented Legal Knowledge Lookup
A RAG component retrieves relevant statutes, policy clauses, 
and court-ruling excerpts from a curated knowledge base to  
ground generated insights in actual legal context, rather than 
presenting  purely  statistical  output.  When  the  correlation 
engine flags a governance bottleneck — for instance, an  
unusually high rate of boundary disputes in a sub-district —  
the  RAG  layer  retrieves  the  specific  survey-settlement  
provisions and relevant precedent that apply, so the officer  
dashboard can present not just “what is happening” but “what 
the applicable rule says should happen,” closing the gap  
between analytics and administrative action.
D. Correlation and Simulation
Pearson correlation and z-score-based anomaly detection are 
applied  over  regional  aggregates  (processing  delay,  
complaint  density,  staffing  ratio)  to  flag  statistically  
significant  governance  bottlenecks,  chosen  for  
interpretability  so  findings  remain  explainable  to  non-
technical policymakers.
X. IMPLEMENTATION
The  prototype,  LandSetu,  was  built  as  a  full-stack  web  
application, with a live demo hosted at:
sih-proto-frontend.vercel.app
The  classification  and  NLP  layer  uses  the  HuggingFace  
Transformers  library  with  a  DistilBERT  backbone;  the  
baseline classifier uses scikit-learn (TF-IDF + linear SVM).  
Data harmonization and entity resolution are implemented  
using  pandas  and  the  RapidFuzz  library.  The  frontend  
(Citizen  Portal,  Officer  Dashboard,  Policy  Dashboard)  is 
built as a Next.js/React application, deployed on Vercel, with 
the  AI/analytics  services  designed  to  run  as  a  separate  
backend layer. Training for the classifier was performed on a 
single GPU-enabled environment; total training time was  
under 20 minutes over the small pilot development batch.
System Requirements
•  Frontend: Next.js/React, Tailwind CSS, deployed on 
Vercel.
•  Backend/AI services: Python 3.10+, HuggingFace 
Transformers, scikit-learn, pandas, RapidFuzz, FastAPI 
(planned for production integration).
•  Storage: relational store for structured land records; 
vector store for the RAG legal knowledge base.
•  Minimum hardware for the classifier: single GPU (or 
CPU-only for inference at reduced throughput), 8 GB 
RAM.
•  Browser-based access for both the Citizen Portal and 
Officer Dashboard, requiring no additional client-side 
installation.

On  this  small  pilot  sample,  LandSetu's  NLP  pipeline  
improved classification accuracy by 15 percentage points  
over the keyword/rule-based baseline (83.3% vs. 68.3%),  
with the largest relative gains in the Boundary Issue and  
Survey Error categories, where free-text descriptions vary  
more  and  are  harder  for  keyword  rules  to  capture.  The  
confusion  matrix  (Fig.  5)  shows  most  misclassifications  
occur between the semantically related Mutation Delay and  
Boundary  Issue  categories,  suggesting  overlapping  
vocabulary  in  citizen  narratives.  On  the  document-
digitization  side,  Table  IV  shows  OCR  field-extraction  
accuracy  ranging  from  80.0%  (Mutation  Status,  often  
handwritten  in  legacy  records)  to  90.0%  (Owner  Name,  
typically printed), indicating that structured fields are already 
extractable  with  reasonable  reliability  while  handwritten  
annotations remain a challenge. Table III makes clear that not 
every module is at the same maturity level: the grievance  
portal and officer dashboard are functionally implemented,  
while  geospatial  alignment,  RAG  retrieval,  and  policy  
simulation remain at partial/prototype stage — an honest  
reflection of a hackathon-stage build rather than a finished  
production system.

XII. NOVELTY AND UNIQUE CONTRIBUTIONS
•  Unlike prior grievance-analytics tools, LandSetu 
explicitly joins structured land records with unstructured 
grievance text at the parcel level via entity resolution, 
rather than treating grievances as a standalone ticketing 
stream.
•  Integration of OCR-based digitization directly into the 
analytical pipeline allows legacy paper records 
(Jamabandi, Khasra) to enter the same harmonized 
schema as digital-native records.
•  A retrieval-augmented legal knowledge (RAG) layer 
grounds generated insights in actual statutes and 
precedent, a feature absent from all reviewed comparison 
systems (Table I).
•  A policy-impact simulation module is designed to let 
administrators test the projected effect of a process 
change before implementing it, moving beyond 
descriptive dashboards toward decision support.
•  A real-time synchronization loop (Fig. 3) connects 
citizen input directly to officer action, rather than routing 
grievances through a static, periodically-updated report.
XIII. CONCLUSION
This  paper  presented  LandSetu,  an  AI-driven  land  
governance intelligence platform that harmonizes structured  
land  records,  OCR-digitized  legacy  documents,  and  
unstructured  citizen  grievance  narratives  through  entity  
resolution,  geospatial  alignment,  and  transformer-based  
NLP,  further  supported  by  retrieval-augmented  legal  
knowledge lookup and policy-impact simulation. An early  
prototype  demonstrated  that  this  combined  approach  
achieves  meaningfully  higher  grievance-classification  
performance (83.3% accuracy on a small pilot sample) than a 
conventional  keyword-based  baseline  (68.3%),  while  its  
architecture  is  explicitly  designed  to  surface  the  kind  of 
correlated, policy-actionable insight that purely descriptive  
dashboards  cannot  provide.  While  current  results  are  
preliminary and drawn from a limited pilot sample, they  
suggest that integrating previously siloed land-governance  
data  streams  through  AI  can  meaningfully  support  
transparent, evidence-based policy decision-making.
XIV. LIMITATIONS
•  The evaluation sample (n = 60 grievances, 20 scanned 
documents) is small and representative/synthetic rather 
than a live, production-scale government feed; results are 
preliminary.
•  The current prototype supports primarily English-
language grievance text; most Indian citizen grievances 
are filed in regional languages, which the present model 
does not yet handle.
•  Several modules (geospatial alignment, RAG retrieval, 
policy simulation) remain at partial/prototype maturity, 
as shown in Table III.
•  The correlation analysis identifies statistical association, 
not causation, and requires domain-expert validation 
before direct policy action.
•  The framework has not yet been tested for computational 
scalability against a full state-level land-records 
database.
XV. FUTURE SCOPE
•  Extend the NLP layer with multilingual/vernacular 
language models to support grievances filed in Hindi and 
other regional languages.
•  Integrate directly with live DILRMP/ULPIN data feeds 
and state grievance-redressal portals for real-time 
deployment at scale.
•  Complete the geospatial alignment, RAG legal-retrieval, 
and policy-simulation modules from prototype to 
production maturity.
•  Add fairness and bias auditing to ensure the platform 
does not disproportionately flag or overlook specific 
regions or demographic groups.
•  Validate correlation and simulation findings through 
field studies in partnership with district revenue 
administration offices.
